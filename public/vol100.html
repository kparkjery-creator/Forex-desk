Read this first

Volatility 100 is a computer-generated random price series, so no news or fundamentals give anyone a guaranteed edge, and most retail traders lose money on it. This guide teaches how to trade it with discipline: limit every loss, trade with the trend, and avoid the habits that wipe out accounts.

It is general education, not financial advice, and it cannot promise profit. Practise on a demo account first.

The guide assumes you trade on a phone with MT5, using the H1 chart for direction and the M15 chart for entries. The worked examples come from your own charts on 14 Nov 2026.

Chapter 1: What Volatility 100 is

Volatility 100 is built to keep a constant 100% volatility and to print a new price every 2 seconds, as the description on your chart says. It keeps moving all day, including weekends: your 14 Nov chart is a Saturday.

Because the series is random, you cannot predict each move. What you can control is how much you lose when you are wrong. That is why this guide puts risk first and entries second:

  • Risk 1% of your account or less on each trade.
  • Always place a stop loss.
  • Only take trades where the reward is at least twice the risk.
  • Stop trading for the day after a set number of losses.

Traders who survive on Vol 100 mostly do these four things. Traders who blow up usually break them.

Chapter 2: Reading the trend

A trend is the direction price keeps moving in, and the cleanest way to read it is by the swings.

  • Uptrend: each high and each low is higher than the one before.
  • Downtrend: each high and each low is lower than the one before.
  • Pullback: a dip against the bigger trend. It is a pause, not a reversal, until price breaks the last swing low.

Use two timeframes. Read the direction on H1, then look for your entry on M15. Trading M15 moves against the H1 direction is how most small losses pile up.

In your 14 Nov charts, H1 climbed from about 514 to 577 with higher lows, so the bigger trend was up. On M15, price pulled back to about 549 and then bounced. That is a pullback inside an uptrend.

On your own H1 chart the lows rise from about 509 to about 531 to about 549. As long as price holds above the last low, the uptrend is intact, and a pullback is a pause, not a reversal.

Chapter 3: Support and resistance

Support is a price area where buyers stepped in before, a floor. Resistance is an area where sellers stepped in, a ceiling. On Volatility 100 they are zones, not exact lines: price often spikes through a level and then reverses.

Your 17:13 chart shows it. Price spiked to about 579 and later to about 577, both well past 571.28, and fell back under it each time. Treating 571.28 as one exact price would have stopped you out or trapped you.

How to trade levels that overshoot:

  • Draw a zone a few points wide around each level, not a single line.
  • Count a break only when a candle closes beyond the zone. A wick through it does not count.
  • Put stops beyond the zone, not on the line, and size the lot for that wider stop (Chapter 6).
  • Wait for a reaction at the zone, a rejection or a hold, instead of entering the moment price touches it.
  • When resistance breaks and holds, the retest of the old level is the safer entry.

The levels you drew on 14 Nov were 539.24, about 549, about 565, 571.28 and 588.91.

Chapter 4: The three moving averages

Your chart shows three moving averages. Their periods are whatever you set in the indicator settings, so check them. What matters is their roles:

  • Blue (fast): follows price closely and shows short-term momentum.
  • Orange (medium): the line price often pulls back to in a healthy trend.
  • Purple (slow): the long-term floor or ceiling. Price far above it in a rising purple line means a strong uptrend.

How to read them:

  • Price above all three, with all three sloping up, is bullish. Price below all three, sloping down, is bearish.
  • Blue crossing above orange is an early sign that momentum is turning up.
  • In an uptrend, pullbacks that touch the orange or blue lines and hold are the entries to look for.

Moving averages lag behind price. Never trade a crossover alone: confirm it with the trend on H1 and a nearby support or resistance level.

Chapter 5: Stop loss, target and risk-to-reward

Before you enter, decide three prices: where you get in, where you are proven wrong (the stop loss), and where you take profit (the target).

  • Stop loss: just beyond the last swing low for a buy, or the last swing high for a sell. If price reaches it, the idea is wrong and you accept the loss.
  • Target: the next resistance for a buy, or the next support for a sell.
  • Reward-to-risk: the distance to the target divided by the distance to the stop. Take trades at 2 or better.

Never move your stop further away to avoid a loss. Moving it closer to lock in profit is fine.

Because Volatility 100 overshoots levels, put the stop beyond the whole zone, not on its edge, and size the lot for that wider stop.

Chapter 6: Position sizing, the 1% rule

Lot size decides how much a losing trade costs, and on Vol 100 it is what ends most accounts. Work it out before every trade, from the stop distance, never the other way around.

Risk amount = Account × 1%
Loss allowed per point = Risk amount ÷ Stop distance (points)

Worked example: with a $100 account, 1% is $1. If your stop is 3 points away, you can lose $1 ÷ 3 = about $0.33 per point. If your stop is 7 points away, only about $0.14 per point. A wider stop means a smaller lot.

Then open your broker's contract specification for Volatility 100 to see what 1 lot is worth per point, and pick the lot size that keeps the loss per point at or under your allowed figure.

If even the smallest lot loses more than 1% at your stop distance, skip the trade or wait for a tighter, still valid stop. Do not break the 1% rule to make the trade fit.

Chapter 7: The step-by-step entry plan

Follow these steps in order. If any step fails, you do not trade.

  1. On H1, mark the trend direction and the main support and resistance zones.
  2. Only look for trades in the H1 direction. If H1 is unclear, stay out.
  3. Drop to M15 and wait for a pullback to the orange or blue line, or to a broken level, that holds.
  4. Decide the stop (beyond the last swing) and the target (the next level) before entering.
  5. Calculate reward-to-risk. If it is under 2, skip the trade.
  6. Size the lot so the stop loss equals 1% of your account or less (Chapter 6).
  7. Enter, place the stop and target, and leave the trade alone.
  8. Log it in your journal (Chapter 10).

Chapter 8: Daily rules and mindset

Write these rules down and treat them as non-negotiable. They protect you on the days your judgement is worst.

  • Stop for the day after 2 to 3 losses in a row, or a 3% loss on the day.
  • Take at most 2 to 3 trades a day. Fewer, cleaner trades beat constant trading.
  • Never trade to win back a loss. That is revenge trading, and it is the biggest account killer.
  • Never add to a losing trade or remove your stop.
  • Do not trade when you are tired, angry or in a rush.
  • Close the app after your trades are placed. Watching every tick pushes you to interfere.

A losing trade that followed your plan is a good trade. A winning trade that broke your rules is a bad one, because it teaches you to break them again.

Chapter 9: Common mistakes and the pre-trade checklist

The mistakes that cost the most are rarely about the entry:

  • Oversized lots.
  • Moving or removing the stop loss.
  • Averaging down on a loser.
  • Overtrading.
  • Trading against the H1 trend.
  • Buying right under resistance, or selling right above support.

Chapter 12: When to enter

The best time to enter is a place on the chart, not a time on the clock. Volatility 100 is computer-generated, never closes and does not follow news or market sessions, so no hour of the day is known to be better. Judge by where price is and whether your checklist passes.

Good entries:

  1. A pullback to the blue or orange line that holds, with H1 trending the same way (Chapter 7).
  2. A retest of a broken level: a candle closes beyond the zone, then price comes back and holds (Chapter 3).
  3. A rejection at the edge of a range with enough room to the next level, taken with a smaller target and a smaller lot.

Timing rules:

  • Enter after a candle closes, not in the middle of it. The price tag shows the time left in the candle. Moves in the middle of a candle are often wicks that vanish.
  • After a big spike candle, wait for one or two candles to close before deciding.
  • To avoid chasing, place a limit order at your planned price and set the stop loss on the same order, so the trade is protected from the moment it opens.
  • Check the room: the next level must be at least twice your stop distance away.

Skip the trade when:

  • Price is in the middle of a range.
  • You would be chasing a candle that has already run a long way.
  • The trade goes against H1 and you have no strong reason.
  • Any box on the checklist is empty.

Chapter 13: When to exit and manage a trade

Decide the exit before you enter. Write down four prices: entry, stop, halfway point and target.

How a trade can end:

  • Target hit: take the profit. Do not move the target further away because you feel greedy.
  • Stop hit: accept the loss. It was planned.
  • The reason is gone: if a candle closes beyond the zone against you, or H1 flips, close early instead of hoping.
  • Time runs out: choose a limit before you enter, for example 8 candles on M30 (4 hours). If price has not moved toward your target by then, reassess or close.

Managing the trade in between:

  • At the halfway point, move the stop to your entry (break-even), so the trade can no longer lose money. The trade-off: on a noisy market a small retrace can stop you out at zero before the real move. That is a fair price for safety.
  • Never move the stop further from price, and never remove it.
  • Optional: close half the trade at the halfway point and let the rest run to the target.
  • Set price alerts at the halfway point and the target, then close the app.

Chapter 14: When to add another position

Adding to a trade is the most dangerous move in this book, because it is how small losses become large ones. Follow these rules exactly.

Never add to a losing trade. Do not "average down". If the first trade is against you, the market is telling you the idea may be wrong. Take the planned loss.

You may add only to a winner, and only when all of these are true:

  1. The first trade is in profit and its stop is already at break-even (Chapter 13).
  2. A new, separate setup has appeared. It needs its own entry, stop, target and a passed checklist.
  3. The new trade's risk plus the first trade's risk is no more than 1% of the account. Because the first stop is at break-even, the first trade now risks nothing.
  4. There is room to the next level, at least twice the new stop distance.
  5. You have two positions at most.

If you cannot tick every box, do nothing. Missing a second trade costs you nothing. A bad second trade can cost the whole day.

Example. You buy at 560.5 with a stop at 557.5. Price rises to 566 and you move the stop to 560.5. If price then pulls back to the blue line, holds, and gives a fresh trigger on M30, you may place a second small buy with its own stop. If price is simply higher and you feel excited, that is not a setup, so you do not add.

Chapter 15: When to stay away from the chart

The market is open all day and all night. You do not have to be. Staying away is a skill, and the best traders do it most of the time.

Close the app when:

  • Price is in the middle of the range, far from any level, and no setup is showing.
  • You have hit your daily limit: 2 or 3 losses, or 3% down. Stop for the day, even if you feel sure the next one will win.
  • You just had a big win. Excitement makes people take poor trades.
  • You are tired, angry or in a hurry. Anger after a loss leads to revenge trades.
  • You cannot check all three timeframes (H4, H1, M30).
  • A huge spike candle has just printed. Wait for a few candles to close.
  • The next level is too close for a 2 : 1 trade.
  • You are bored. The market being open is not a reason to trade.

Before you leave a trade open:

  • Check that the stop loss and take profit are on the order.
  • Check your phone battery and signal. A trade with no stop and no connection is the worst case.
  • Set price alerts for your halfway point and target.

A simple daily routine. Look at the charts at fixed times, for example morning and evening. Spend 10 minutes: H4, then H1, then M30. Write down one of three decisions: trade, wait or stay away. Then close the app. Every "stay away" day is also a successful day if it kept your account safe.

Chapter 17: Our market approach to Volatility 100

This chapter pulls the whole book into one approach you can follow every day.

What kind of market this is

  • Volatility 100 is a computer-generated series with constant volatility. It has no news, no sessions and no opening or closing times, and it never stops.
  • Because it is random in the short run, no signal is certain. The aim is not to predict. The aim is to take only trades where the loss is small and the reward is at least twice the loss, and to repeat that many times.
  • Levels act as zones. Price often goes through them and returns, so stops go beyond the zone, not on the line.
  • Moves can be fast and large, so position size comes from the stop distance and the 1% rule, never from a feeling.

Step 1: name the market state. Then follow the plan for that state.

Step 2: run the three-timeframe routine (Chapter 11). H4 gives the map, H1 the direction, M30 the trigger. If D1 is available, glance at it for large ceilings and floors before you set a target.

Step 3: pass the checklist (Chapter 12). Trigger, room of at least twice the stop, risk of 1% or less, daily limit not reached, and you are rested.

Step 4: manage the trade by the plan (Chapter 13). Stop and target on the order, stop to break-even at the halfway point, no widening, close if the reason is gone.

Step 5: record and review. Write every trade in a journal: date, state, setup, entry, stop, target, result, and one lesson. Every Sunday, read the week together and count how many trades followed the rules.

Chapter 18: The bigger picture and the top-down strategy

Every lesson so far has used the small charts (M30, M15) to find the entry. This chapter zooms out. The higher time frames (H4 and D1) do not give you the entry. They give you the context: where the market has been, where the big walls are, and how much room there is before price hits one. A trade that looks perfect on M15 can be a poor trade if a big wall sits only a few points above it.

18.1 Why zoom out first

  • Context. Is price falling, rising or sitting still on the big chart? Trading with the big move is easier than trading against it.
  • Room. The small chart cannot show you a big ceiling that is just off its screen. The H4 chart can.
  • Level history. A level that was touched three times over two weeks is stronger than one drawn from a single wick.
  • Calm. On H4 one candle is four hours. You see fewer, clearer decisions and less noise.

18.10 Rules for drawing a good zone

  1. Two touches or more. One wick is a hint, not a zone.
  2. Draw a band, not a line. Use the wick-to-body width of the touches, about 3 to 5 points on this index.
  3. Start on H4, refine on M15. Find the zone on the big chart, then zoom in to place its edges.
  4. Check both directions. A level that stopped price from above and from below is a pivot and is strong.
  5. Do not draw a line where price never stopped. 604.81 was drawn above the real high, which is why it looked close but never reacted.
  6. Redraw after every swing. Delete lines that have not been touched for weeks. Keep about five to seven on the screen.
  7. Flip rule: when price closes beyond a zone and the retest holds, the old ceiling becomes the new floor (577.94 today).

Chapter 23: The mirror image, a buy plan for an uptrend

The 22 Nov session was a sell trend: lower highs, price under the averages, sells on weak bounces. Volatility 100 can also trend up, and then every rule flips and nothing else changes. This chapter is a template built from our rules, not a trade seen on a chart. Practise it on the demo account before it is ever used with real money.

23.2 The buy routine, step by step

  1. Map (D1, H4): price is above the averages and the averages slope up. If not, there is no buy-trend plan. A buy against the trend is a small, quick bounce trade at most.
  2. Direction (H1): higher lows, price holding above the averages.
  3. Trigger (M30): wait for a pullback to the blue and orange averages, or to an old resistance that is now support, and for a bullish candle to close there.
  4. Score the three timeframes. All agree is full size, two is small or skip, one or none is stay away.
  5. Write the plan before the trade: entry, stop below the zone (not on the line), first target at the next resistance, at least 2:1, with room of at least twice the stop.
  6. Size the lot: lot = risk in dollars divided by the stop in points (1% rule).
  7. Manage it: stop to break-even at halfway, partial profit at the first hurdle, then trail the stop under the latest pullback low. You may tighten a stop. Never widen it.
  8. Exit rules: a time stop (for example 8 M30 candles with nothing happening), or an M30 close below the stop zone.

23.4 Mistakes that are the same on both sides

  • Chasing the first spike when price is far from the averages.
  • Putting the stop on the line instead of beyond the zone.
  • Closing by hand before the target. On 22 Nov two exits made that way left money on the table (Chapter 22).
  • Adding a third position, or buying and selling together (hedging).
  • Trading past the daily loss limit.

No result is guaranteed on either side. A plan that is followed can be measured, and the demo goal of 50 trades is how it gets tested.

One-page cheat sheet

Before any trade, answer yes to all of these:

  1. H4: do I know the big levels, and is there room (at least 2x my stop) to the next one?
  2. H1: is the trend the same way as my trade?
  3. M30: has a candle closed with a real trigger, such as a pullback holding or a retest?
  4. Is price away from the middle of the range?
  5. Are my stop, target and halfway price written down before I enter?
  6. Is my risk 1% or less, and am I under my daily limit (2 to 3 losses, 3% down)?

Score: 3 of 3 = trade. 2 of 3 = small or skip. 0 or 1 = stay away.

Bigger picture (H4): mark swing high and low, five levels, slow-line direction and side, and which side has more room. Grade the trade A (all frames agree), B (half size, short target) or C (skip).

During the trade: at halfway move the stop to entry. Never widen the stop. Take profit at the target. Close if the reason is gone or the time limit passes.

Adding: only to a winner whose stop is at break-even, with a new setup. Maximum two positions.

Stop for the day when: limit hit, tired, angry, after a big win, or just bored.
